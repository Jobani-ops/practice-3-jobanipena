Piet Mondrain

Was a Dutch painter and art theoretician, who is regarded as one of the greatest artists of the 20th century. He was one of the pioneers of 20th-century abstract art, as he changed his artistic direction from figurative painting to an increasingly abstract style, until he reached a point where his artistic vocabulary was taken down to simple geometric elements.

Piet Mondrian painted “Composition with Red Blue and Yellow” in 1930. It marks a subtle turning point in the evolution of his distinctive, singular style of painting, which he called Neo-Plasticism. His visual voice came about through a meticulous, daily striving towards complete abstraction, which he believed was a way to express a universal purity through the plastic arts. It was in 1920, the same year he published his book Le Néo-Plasticisme, that Mondrian painted his first grid paintings, featuring the black horizontal and vertical lines and rectangular fields of pure color for which he is now most known. Early on in his exploration of this unique position, Mondrian experimented with multiple colors and tones, and explored a wide variety of configurations for both his canvases and for the shapes within his compositions. By the time of his death in 1944, he had arrived at a fantastically complex and mature vision, embodied by his masterpiece, “Broadway Boogie Woogie.” It was in the middle of that evolution that he painted “Composition with Red Blue and Yellow.” It may not be obvious why this work is remarkable, or why it marks an important turning point. But there are some aspects of it that clearly set it apart from so many other paintings Mondrian made, and which signal a point in his development when Mondrian himself was becoming transformed.

Less until it holds

Mondrian started as a landscape painter. Trees, windmills, and dunes, painted in the ordinary way. In Paris he saw what Picasso and Braque were doing and began breaking his subjects into planes and lines. Within a few years the subjects were gone and only the lines remained.

He called the result Neoplasticism, and he set rules for it: only horizontal and vertical lines, only the three primary colors plus black, white and grey, and no symmetry. The rules were not a limit. They were the point. With everything else removed, the relationships between the parts had to carry the whole picture..

The 1930 Composition is one of the clearest examples. One red square takes most of the canvas. A small blue block and a smaller yellow one sit at the bottom corners. The black lines are not all the same width, and the white areas are not all the same white. It looks simple from across a room and turns out not to be.

The lines

Only horizontal and vertical. Mondrian painted them by hand, and up close the edges are not perfectly straight. They stop short of the canvas edge in places, which keeps the grid from feeling like a cage.
